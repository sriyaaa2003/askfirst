# askfirst

When should a replenishment system order on its own, and when should it ask a planner?

askfirst forecasts weekly demand for every store-item in the Walmart M5 data, puts a calibrated
interval around each forecast, turns that into an order, and then decides which orders a
planner with limited time should look at. It measures how much each extra review is worth
(the "autonomy frontier") against simple rules, instead of assuming that "ask when unsure" works.

All numbers below are generated by the code and listed in [results/results.md](results/results.md).

![autonomy frontier](results/autonomy_frontier.png)

## What it found

The test covers 48 weeks and 1,460,477 weekly ordering decisions (30,490 store-item series).

1. **Asking where the model is unsure does worse than asking at random.** Sending the 10% of
   decisions with the widest relative interval to a planner captures 6.0% of the saving a full
   review would give; random captures 10.0%. Wide intervals sit on slow, intermittent items
   where an error costs little.
2. **What works is value at stake.** Ranking decisions by expected cost of acting alone, or by
   the simulated expected value of a review, captures 40.0% of the all-review saving at a 10%
   budget (95% CI 39.5% to 40.5%, resampling test weeks). Ranking by price times forecast
   captures 38.1%. The Monte Carlo score is better by 1.9 points (CI 1.6 to 2.1), which is
   real but small. An oracle that knows the true benefit reaches 52.6%, so most of the
   remaining gap is not closed by this method.
3. **The Monte Carlo score is almost the same as plain expected cost.** With a planner who
   removes a fixed share of the error, the two scores are proportional (Spearman 0.9996, and
   a unit test shows why). A planner who helps mostly where a price drop or event is visible
   breaks that (Spearman 0.887), but the capture barely moves (40.2% against 40.0%).
4. **Errors are spread out.** Half of the all-review saving needs 17% of decisions reviewed,
   80% needs 47%. At a 5% budget that is 1,522 reviews per week, about 25 planner hours.
5. **How much to review depends on what a review costs.** With one review priced at 0.67
   (1 minute at 40 per hour) the best share is 60% of decisions and the net saving is 28.9%;
   at four times that price the best share is 9% and the saving 10.0%. Scaling the shortage or
   surplus costs by 0.5 or 2 moves the best share between 42% and 74%. The ranking of the rules
   does not change in any of those runs.

Forecast quality is modest: WAPE 0.363 against 0.385 for a 4-week average. The 80% interval
covers 79.6% of test demand overall and 78.6% in the highest-value decile, so unlike
[tailwatch](https://github.com/sriyaaa2003/tailwatch) the intervals hold up in the risky rows.
The order-up-to level is off for the three food departments: the target service level is
42.9% and the achieved level is 49.1% to 50.3%, because demand drifts between the calibration
and test weeks.

## How it works

1. `data.py` aggregates daily M5 sales to weeks and joins prices (a week without a price means
   the item is not on sale and is not a decision).
2. `features.py` builds lags, rolling statistics, price, event and SNAP features; a test checks
   that nothing from week w or later reaches the features of week w.
3. `forecast.py` trains one global gradient-boosted Poisson model (weeks 52 to 189), calibrates
   on weeks 190 to 224 and tests on weeks 225 to 272. Residuals are scaled by a local scale known
   before the week and conformal quantiles are taken per department.
4. `policy.py` orders with the newsvendor rule (forecast plus the conformal quantile at the
   critical ratio), simulates a planner, and scores each decision by Monte Carlo (200 draws
   from the calibration residuals) for the expected cost of acting alone and the expected value
   of a review.
5. `experiment.py` ranks decisions inside each planning week, applies a review budget, and
   records cost against budget for six ranking rules, three planner qualities and one
   context-aware planner.

## Assumptions you should not forget

- **The costs are assumptions, not Walmart numbers.** Lost margin 30% of price; surplus cost
  40% of price for foods (spoilage) and 8% otherwise; a review costs 0.67. See `configs/default.toml`.
- **The planner is simulated.** It removes a share of the model error. There is no real
  planner, so the savings are what a planner of that quality would give, not a measurement of
  people. The simulated planner sees part of the realised demand, so this is an upper bound on
  what a review can do.
- **One period, no stock carry-over.** Each week is an independent newsvendor decision with a
  one-week lead time. Real replenishment has inventory, lead times and pack sizes.
- **The data is a mirror.** The official M5 download was blocked and Kaggle needs a login, so
  the three files come from the Hugging Face upload `denephew/M5_Forecasting`. They match the
  published structure (30,490 series, 3,049 items, 10 stores, 1,913 days, 6,841,121 price rows).
- The model is trained once and not refit over the test weeks.

## Run it

```bash
pip install -e ".[dev]"
python -m askfirst.experiment --config configs/default.toml   # about 2 minutes
python -m askfirst.report                                     # writes results/results.md
./run_sensitivity.sh                                          # cost-assumption variants
pytest                                                        # 16 tests
```

Put `calendar.csv`, `sales_train_validation.csv` and `sell_prices.csv` from the M5 forecasting
data in `data/raw/`.
